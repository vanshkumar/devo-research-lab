# A prospective test of neural learning-rule forecasts during mouse task acquisition

Working scientific draft, 29 October 2026. Unpublished; not submitted. This draft reports two completed mouse forecasting campaigns. Its contribution and suitability for publication remain subject to scientific review.

## Abstract

Inferred behavioral learning rules can predict choices and generate learning trajectories. Their utility for prefix-limited, prospective acquisition forecasting remains an empirical question. We tested published neural learning-rule architectures on public mouse visual-discrimination histories, calibrating each forecast with 1,000 task opportunities and generating choices, feedback and an adaptive curriculum for 3,000 further opportunities. An initial study used 40 full-horizon held-out animals, including 19 from two reserved laboratories. A recurrent model added as a postvalidation challenge improved marginal distributional prediction, without meeting the original individual-forecast criteria. Its encouraging cohort mean motivated a second test: the unchanged recurrent model and baselines were frozen before prefixes from 24 previously unused, format-excluded animals were examined. At exactly ±100% contrast, recurrent population-curve error was 6.97 percentage points (pp) versus 2.28 pp for empirical growth; growth's paired advantage was 4.69 pp (95% animal-bootstrap interval 2.85–5.67). The recurrent model overpredicted every future bin. Its population criteria failed, although bias intervals overlapped the 5 pp tolerance boundary and the gate had low acceptance probability for correctly centered noisy forecasts. A postvalidation diagnostic on these same 24 animals achieved 0.63 pp population error with actual preceding histories and stimuli; this was not a prospective forecast or an isolation of feedback information. Separately, a future-only session-state perturbation reduced autonomous error to 2.12 pp. These results establish a prospective limitation of the tested recipe and sensitivity to generative assumptions, not an impossibility of transferable rules or a unique missing mechanism. No real external-policy outcome was examined.

## Introduction

Inferring how animals update behavior could connect trial-level learning mechanisms to forecasts of acquisition. Neural parameterizations offer flexible update rules, and existing work demonstrates prediction of held-out animals and future choices using their actual preceding experience [1]. Related work fits dynamics across curricula and already evaluates prediction likelihood along trajectories that sample decisions from the inferred generative model [2, Appendix Figure 9]. Thus generated-choice evaluation itself is prior work. Our practical question concerns a frozen across-animal forecast: after an early behavioral prefix, does a rule predict subsequent acquisition usefully against strong baselines when it also generates the intervening adaptive curriculum?

The distinction matters because a model used to compare curricula must generate the experiences through which its simulated animal learns. In an adaptive task, choices affect subsequent stimuli and feedback; errors in the learner, observation process or trainer can compound together. A good conditional predictor may nevertheless be a good generative model: with correct full-history conditional probabilities and a correct environment, the probability chain rule supplies the joint law. Conditional success alone does not establish that those requirements hold under the histories generated by an estimated model.

We report two sequential tests using public International Brain Laboratory acquisition histories [3]. The first evaluated autonomous individual forecasts against persistence and an empirical exposure/growth model. A stronger recurrent architecture was subsequently fitted on the original development set and evaluated as an explicitly postvalidation challenge. Its old-cohort mean forecast motivated a second, prospectively frozen population test on previously unused animals. The second test is the principal prospective result; it does not retroactively change the first test's endpoint or validation status. Our contribution is this bounded assessment of autonomous forecast utility, rather than a new neural architecture, a first demonstration of held-out behavioral prediction, or a validated curriculum intervention.

## Design and methods

### Data, allocation and information boundaries

Here “prospective” means that consequential analysis choices and forecast files were frozen before access to the relevant outcome values in existing public records. This was an outcome-access-controlled retrospective analysis, not prospective collection of new animals or a registered prospective cohort study.

The public standard-curriculum cache contains 154 training animals. Initially, 128 had trial tables for every indexed session in the immediately supported format. Metadata-only allocation assigned 74 to development, nested into 62 fitting and 12 checkpoint/calibration animals, and reserved 54: 27 unfamiliar animals in development laboratories and 27 from Hofer and Witten laboratories. No eventual learning criterion selected animals. Forty-eight reserved animals had a 1,000-opportunity prefix and at least one future bin; 40 had the primary 3,000-opportunity future, including 21 unfamiliar-animal and 19 unfamiliar-laboratory cases. Shorter histories remained in disposition and secondary analyses. Indexed prefixes were not certified complete biological first exposure.

The second study recovered the 26 animals previously excluded solely because their sessions mixed Parquet and legacy array formats. Header, footer and metadata reconstruction identified 24 with the full prefix and future: Hofer 10, MrsicFlogel 6, Angelaki 5, Churchland 2 and Cortex 1. NYU-21 supplied four future bins and was secondary; SWC_007 had only 517 opportunities and its values remained unopened. This was a format-selected cohort from laboratories already represented in the research program, not a random sample or 24 independent environments.

In both studies, autonomous forecasts received the first 1,000 task opportunities, including omissions, and the realized future session opportunity/boundary vector. They generated subsequent stimuli, choices, feedback, coefficient updates, corrections and curriculum promotions. Real future choices, rewards and adaptive stimulus sequences were withheld until forecast files had been saved. Because future opportunities were supplied, the estimand conditions on realized exposure and session boundaries; it does not forecast attendance, calendar time or outcome-dependent stopping.

The first study's scoring conversion decoded complete indexed histories of its 48 forecastable reserved animals after forecasts were saved. Those histories are consumed even where later rows were not scored. The second study returned only the first 4,000 rows of its 24 primary animals and first 3,000 of NYU-21, totaling 99,000 unique rows including prefixes. Its later rows remained unexamined. Twenty protected animals from a separate fixed-curriculum release [4] remained sealed throughout both studies.

### Models and departures from the source analysis

The [methods appendix](METHODS_APPENDIX.md) specifies the implemented choice features, recurrent update, prefix uncertainty, donor-growth comparator and exact population target without requiring the reader to infer them from source code.

The original candidate set comprised additive DNNGLM rules with two ReLU layers, source-style REINFORCE with a fitted reward baseline, and pure reward REINFORCE. The implementation reconstructed the published architecture and recurrence [1] on a new allocation; it was not a replication of the paper's exact 12-animal numerical experiment. The source notebook drops omissions. Here omissions remained exposure opportunities and observable failures, using a common development-derived engagement/omission component for every model. The actor used binary correctness; reward volume and wheel gain were not active learning inputs. These choices define the tested forecasting recipe and limit attribution of any failure to the published learning-rule architecture alone.

Persistence used the complete prefix. The growth comparator borrowed subsequent changes from 15 development neighbors matched on prefix behavior, without using a test animal's future. It was intended to capture ordinary acquisition associated with exposure, a stronger comparator than constant skill alone.

After the original source result, a GRU32 recurrent rule with two ReLU layers was fitted only on the same original 62 fitting animals, with checkpoint and variance mapping based on the 12 original calibration animals. Its seed, 1,000-epoch ceiling and evaluation recipe were separately frozen before recurrent source scoring. Its selection was postvalidation and did not restore untouched status to the original holdouts. In the second study, this rule, the growth bank, persistence and trainer were frozen unchanged before any new prefix. Prefix calibration fitted only three initial GLM coefficients by the existing MAP procedure; recurrent memory and uncertainty propagation used the established prefix recipe. Each model generated 512 particles per new animal.

Historical task-code comparisons supported the implemented adaptive correction and promotion rules. They could not recover all operator interventions, body weights or session-specific settings. The simulated trainer therefore represents a documented protocol envelope, rather than a complete reconstruction of each animal's acquisition environment.

### Distinct endpoints and fixed acceptance criteria

The first study scored six 500-opportunity future bins using side-specific high-contrast accuracy at absolute contrast ≥0.5, right-choice share among all trials, and omission share. Omissions counted as failures. Marginal continuous ranked probability score (CRPS), lower being better, was averaged equally across metrics, bins and animals. Right-choice share is a bias-related observable, not standardized psychometric bias. Neither marginal CRPS nor marginal interval coverage establishes joint trajectory calibration. The individual gate required at least 10% and 0.002 absolute CRPS improvement over both strong baselines, at least 10% accuracy-error improvement with mean absolute error (MAE) ≤15 pp, calibration and benefit across both reserved laboratories. The comparison-family screen expanded from four to five candidates before recurrent scoring.

The second study deliberately evaluated a population target: equal-side correct share at exactly ±100% contrast, averaging animals equally within each bin and then the six bins equally. No 0.5-contrast trials entered this endpoint. Every primary side cell was observed, with minimum denominator 32. Its errors are not directly comparable with the first study's individual, ≥0.5 endpoint or multimetric CRPS.

The frozen population gate required animal-t and five-laboratory CR1 cluster 90% intervals for scalar bias wholly inside ±5 pp, mean-curve MAE ≤5 pp, maximum bin error ≤7.5 pp, laboratory absolute bias ≤10 pp, leave-one-laboratory-out absolute bias ≤5 pp, and MAE no more than 1 pp worse than the better baseline. Known-answer controls and independent checks were additional requirements. The cluster sensitivity used five groups and four degrees of freedom, with no claim to exact inference over arbitrary laboratories. Paired animal-bootstrap comparisons used 20,000 fixed-seed resamples and describe this selected laboratory mixture.

### Controls and implementation checks

Known transferable-learner simulations tested whether the first pipeline could detect a useful learning rule; fixed-competence, growth and changing-engagement controls challenged simpler explanations. The second study calibrated its gate using actual laboratory counts and several empirical and synthetic error constructions. Original-byte reconstruction checked targets, signs, denominators and scores independently of production scoring. Independent recurrence and simulation replays checked generated predictions. These checks reduce implementation explanations; biological validity requires the empirical forecasts and appropriate controls, not agreement between software alone.

After both primary studies, an exploratory diagnostic applied the frozen RNN to the same 24 new animals using actual future stimuli and preceding choices/feedback, with predictions computed before each current outcome. It reused only their consumed first 4,000 rows and fitted no new learner. Conditional expectations used the saved central prefix state and original prefix-derived omission recipe; saved autonomous forecasts used 512-particle predictive mixtures. Thus predictive averaging as well as information differed. This was a diagnostic on identical exact-100% targets, not another prospective validation.

## Results

### Individual forecasts improved little over empirical growth

On the original 40 primary animals, DNN32 achieved marginal CRPS 0.08052 and high-contrast MAE 16.38 pp, compared with growth's 0.08097 and 16.28 pp. The CRPS gain was 0.000458, or 0.57%, with paired 95% interval −0.009719 to 0.010584. The postvalidation RNN improved CRPS to 0.07534 and MAE to 15.75 pp. Its CRPS advantage over growth was 0.005636, or 6.96%, with interval −0.001407 to 0.013065 (Figure 1). These intervals allow useful improvement but do not establish it or equivalence. No candidate met the original gate. The RNN was slightly worse than growth in Hofer and better in Witten; two reserved laboratories provide limited environmental replication.

Conditional prediction remained substantially better. On the original cohort, DNN32's next-choice log loss with actual preceding history was 0.527342, versus 0.682517 for a frozen prefix GLM: a gain of 0.155176 nats per choice (95% interval 0.119092–0.193879). RNN log loss was 0.528045. Current responses and rewards affected only later predictions. Diagnostic high-contrast curve errors with actual histories and stimuli were 2.47 pp for DNN32 and 2.30 pp for RNN, compared with autonomous errors of 16.38 and 15.75 pp. Both history and stimulus information differed, including the adaptive sequence; this is not a causal isolation of feedback information.

Autonomous learning was nevertheless generated. From first to last future bin, observed old-cohort mean accuracy rose 52.69% to 68.61%; RNN generated 54.61% to 67.28%, DNN32 54.31% to 73.52%, and growth 52.59% to 62.39%. The encouraging recurrent mean was descriptive and did not substitute for the individual gate. It motivated the new population test.

### The frozen recurrent mean forecast lost prospectively to growth

In the new 24 animals, observed exact-100% accuracy averaged 54.66%. The unchanged RNN predicted 61.63%, growth 56.94%, and persistence 48.28% (Table 1; Figure 2). RNN overpredicted all six future population bins. Its error increased from 3.08 pp in the first bin to 8.87 pp in the last; the maximum error, 9.90 pp, occurred in the fifth bin. Conditional Monte Carlo standard error for its scalar forecast was 0.078 pp, distinct from biological sampling uncertainty.

Growth's mean-curve MAE advantage was 4.692 pp (95% paired animal-bootstrap interval 2.850–5.668); its individual marginal CRPS advantage was 0.008648 (0.003171–0.013889). Persistence's 0.582 pp mean-curve advantage was unresolved, while individual CRPS favored RNN at the point estimate. Thus the result supports growth over this RNN recipe, not superiority of every simple comparator.

Observed-minus-RNN scalar bias was −6.970 pp, with animal 90% interval −8.97 to −4.97 and laboratory-cluster sensitivity −11.07 to −2.87. Both intervals overlap the −5 pp tolerance boundary. The study therefore does not establish an underlying absolute bias greater than 5 pp, and certainly does not establish ±5 pp equivalence. The gate also failed curve, maximum-bin, laboratory, leave-laboratory and baseline criteria. All five laboratory point biases were negative. Leaving out any one laboratory retained a descriptive growth advantage of 2.77–5.02 pp, although the small subgroups do not establish uniform superiority across laboratories. The partial NYU-21 analysis favored RNN at the point estimate and remained secondary.

### Postvalidation tracking on the same 24 animals

The matched diagnostic achieved population-curve MAE 0.628 pp, versus saved autonomous RNN 6.970 pp and growth 2.278 pp (Figure 3). Equal-side individual-curve MAE was 2.497 pp, versus 9.029 and 6.967 pp, respectively. On responding trials, equal-animal conditional log loss was 0.53447 versus 0.66882 for the frozen prefix-static predictor, a gain of 0.13435 nats per choice (exploratory paired 95% interval 0.10878–0.16125). Target counts and prefix values matched exactly; independent pre-update recurrence checks agreed to numerical precision.

This same-cohort, same-endpoint comparison removes a cohort difference from the tracking-versus-autonomous contrast. It shows that the frozen updater can track these animals when supplied actual experience, while its saved autonomous recipe missed their mean. It does not identify why: actual stimuli, adaptive exposure, behavioral histories and central-state versus mixture averaging differ between regimes. Analytic conditional expectations are not complete autonomous trajectory distributions. The diagnostic was selected after validation, leaves both primary results unchanged, and cannot establish a causal benefit of feedback alone.

A [subsequent postvalidation calculation](../../lab/tasks/learning-forecast-information-audit-2026-11-01/RESULT.md), checked by [independent review](../../lab/reviews/learning-forecast-information-audit-2026-11-01/REVIEW.md), matched the original 512 joint prefix particles and generated scoring responses. Conditional population-curve MAE remained 0.619 pp versus 6.970 pp autonomous (difference 6.350 pp; descriptive paired-animal 95% interval 3.622–8.429); the analytic conditional mixture gave 0.628 pp. This excludes the documented central-state/mixture and final-response-sampling difference as an account of the original contrast. A separately predeclared count-weighting diagnostic, however, reduced autonomous population MAE to 1.053 pp and the gap to 0.433 pp by pooling generated correct counts over generated endpoint opportunities within animals and sides. That changes the averaging estimand and earns no prospective success; it exposes consequential count–accuracy coupling. Actual adaptive stimuli, choices, feedback and count distributions remain jointly different, and equal prefix weights are not a future-likelihood-conditioned posterior.

A [subsequent joint count-and-accuracy audit](../../lab/tasks/learning-forecast-count-joint-audit-2026-11-01/RESULT.md) and [independent review](../../lab/reviews/learning-forecast-count-joint-audit-2026-11-01/REVIEW.md) found that, for these frozen forecasts in 24 consumed mice, generated opportunity-count/accuracy coupling makes cohort mean accuracy highly sensitive to the averaging functional. RNN's favorable pooled cohort mean does not establish reliable individual or joint acquisition prediction: its separately declared joint point advantage was 3.617%, with animal/laboratory uncertainty intervals spanning zero, also appeared without correctness, and was accompanied by no resolved marginal-accuracy gain in the predeclared contrary. Accuracy-only CRPS was 0.129503 for RNN versus 0.128924 for growth (growth-minus-RNN −0.000579; paired-animal 95% percentiles −0.013154 to 0.014286); particle Monte Carlo standard error 0.000893 exceeded that difference. The small opposite ordering does not establish growth superiority. These are marginal cell scores, not a full trajectory-dependence test, and differences between score types cannot be subtracted to assign causation. This finite postvalidation evaluation prevents promoting the 1.053 pp pooled summary into a repaired original forecast or a demonstrated learning-rule benefit; no general method or biological/trainer cause was established.

### Gate failure, generative sensitivity and policy extrapolation

The gate was conservative but low powered. Across 20,000 repetitions per control construction, the worst acceptance of a known bias at or beyond ±5 pp was 4.10% (exact Monte Carlo 95% upper limit 4.384%). However, correctly centered empirical source-like forecasts passed only 16.34%, and Gaussian counterparts 18.82%. Failure alone can therefore reflect unresolved precision. Here the paired loss to growth supplies evidence beyond the gate's binary decision.

Generative assumptions remained a substantial objection. After validation, multiplying both coefficients and recurrent memory by 0.8 at each future session boundary reduced new-cohort mean-curve error to 2.12 pp, with maximum bin error 4.09 pp. This used unchanged primary prefix calibration: it was a future-only diagnostic, not a coherent alternate prefix-and-future fit, an estimated biological retention rate or a prospectively earned replacement. It demonstrates that the miss is sensitive to session assumptions. In the original study, coherent 0.8 prefix-and-future retention had not rescued DNN or REINFORCE; those were different models and cohorts.

Before new outcomes were opened, the RNN also predicted a 22.43 pp fixed-minus-adaptive policy difference from the same 24 prefixes (84.07% versus 61.63%). An identical probability probe retained 20.90 pp. Removing correction dynamics in an earlier source-only simulation reduced a 25.34 pp contrast to 6.15 pp, implicating closed-loop trainer behavior in its size. The future-only 0.8 diagnostic retained an 18.42 pp simulated policy difference while changing the predicted fixed-policy mean to 75.21%. None of these numbers is an observed intervention effect. The source gate failed, the real external cohort remained sealed, and exact external custom acquisition-code provenance also remained incomplete.

### What the controls and reconstructions establish

A known transferable learner, fitted with the empirical optimizer, beat growth by 22.6% CRPS in 24 new synthetic source animals and by 10.3% in 24 synthetic fixed-protocol animals. These are positive assay controls, not real external validation. A known session-retention mismatch could make a correct rule fail and could be rescued by supplying the correct assumption, supporting the relevance of generative misspecification without identifying the empirical cause.

Independent original-data reconstruction matched the first study's targets and 1,728 original score rows to numerical precision; recurrent checks added 432 score rows. All 336 main old-source forecasts replayed exactly offline. In the second study, independent checks reproduced all 25 target/count sets and 195 score, interval and gate comparisons, while native row semantics and 36 source-policy forecast replays agreed. These results make tested sign, count, recurrence and serialization errors unlikely explanations. They do not validate unmeasured biological state, task settings or the model's causal interpretation.

## Discussion

The central empirical result is a prospective loss of a frozen recurrent acquisition forecast to an empirical growth baseline in a new, format-selected source cohort. Its importance is bounded but concrete: an encouraging average curve in already examined data did not establish population forecast calibration in unused animals. The earlier study separately showed substantial conditional next-choice prediction and limited individual autonomous utility. Together they motivate evaluating acquisition forecasts under the information actually available when a future trajectory is required.

The closest neural learning-rule work already contributes conditional held-out prediction [1], and related generative work already samples decisions during predictive evaluation [2, Appendix Figure 9]. The remaining distinction is the frozen across-animal, prefix-limited population forecast, adaptive trainer and prospective comparison with growth in previously unused animals; priority for that combination is not established. Descriptive learning-stage inference [5] and neural predictors of acquisition [4] address related questions and are not refuted. Frozen cross-policy empirical transport was an intended further test, but was not completed. The [prior-work clarification](PRIOR_UPDATE.md) records the Figure 9 finding without rewriting the historical campaign audit.

There is no identified unique mechanism for the loss. Errors could arise in the inferred updater, initial-state estimation, omitted reward or movement variables, engagement, session transitions, trainer reconstruction, or their interaction. The large improvement under a future-only session perturbation makes an architecture-only failure interpretation particularly weak. Its postvalidation selection also prevents treating that improvement as a successful new forecast. Correct conditional laws can generate correct joint laws; the present results concern estimated models under particular operational assumptions.

Several limitations constrain generalization. The second cohort was selected by storage format and completion, and included only five laboratories, two with one or two animals. Supplying realized opportunity boundaries may condition on consequences of behavior; the simulator does not identify a causal conditional law for stopping or exposure. The source records do not certify first biological experience. Omission modeling extends the published notebook, and binary correctness omits reward amount and movement. Marginal scores do not validate full trajectories. The original RNN comparison was postvalidation; only its subsequent unchanged population recipe received a new prospective test. Finally, failure to pass a stringent low-power gate is weaker than demonstrating that a tolerance is violated, and the reported intervals do not support equivalence or an underlying bias definitively exceeding 5 pp.

The evidence supports using autonomous forecast tests, strong exposure baselines and explicit generative-assumption challenges before interpreting inferred rules as usable virtual learners. It does not justify a new architecture tournament on these consumed outcomes or treating simulated curriculum gains as measured benefits. A future model would need a coherent treatment of prefix inference and session dynamics followed by genuinely independent forecasting; this is a requirement implied by the present limitations, not a result already obtained here.
